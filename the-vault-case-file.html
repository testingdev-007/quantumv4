00 · Before you start

Why it's built this way

Three deliberate design decisions, and the learning-science reasoning behind each one.

The story is the delivery mechanism, not decoration

A heist with a six-minute clock is what makes "you need somewhere to keep that number" feel like a real problem instead of a definition to memorise. This follows cognitive load theory — working memory is scarce and easily overloaded, so instruction should minimise effort spent on anything other than the concept itself1 — and anchored instruction / situated learning — abstract ideas are learned faster and retained longer when embedded in a concrete, meaningful problem2.

◆ Facilitator note

A 2024 study on narrative-led instructional video found measurably better short-term retention than lecture-style delivery of the same content3 — that study wasn't run on adult career-changers learning to code, so treat it as support for the shape of the design, not proof this exact audience behaves identically.

Every blank is filled by choosing, never by typing or dragging

The wrong choices are real wrong choices with real consequences on screen — picking x as a variable name still runs, and the activity explains exactly why nobody will thank you for it in three weeks. This is the worked-example effect: novices learn a procedure better from studying correct and incorrect worked steps than from unconstrained trial and error4. It's also why every gate in the build (Valid/Invalid buttons, Run buttons, Next-room buttons) stays visibly and honestly locked until its real condition is met, rather than looking clickable and silently refusing.

Nobody can be hard-locked out by the clock

If the six minutes run out, Okonjo buys ninety seconds with a line of dialogue — and if that also runs out, Vale buys a further thirty as a named, on-screen favour. This is now a hard guarantee, not a two-strike allowance: if a learner needs more rescuing than that (an early build genuinely let this happen, and a tester reached negative time by Act 2–3 — unacceptable for a first-time coder), the clock keeps rescuing indefinitely — a rotating pool of small, randomised, in-fiction excuses ("the guards just clocked on for a coffee break," "a pigeon set off a motion sensor two floors up," and more) each buy a further 20–39 seconds, never repeating twice in a row. The clock is structurally incapable of going negative on any playthrough. A tool for absolute beginners who describe themselves as anxious about technology cannot have a failure state that ends the activity. The tension is motivational, never punitive.

Every job ends by naming the career it feeds into

After each task, a "job complete" screen names the construct just used and a specific bank tech role that genuinely runs on it (full detail in each task's own section, and in §06). Tying new material to its future personal relevance is a documented utility-value intervention — one of the few motivational interventions with experimental evidence of improving both interest and measured performance in a technical subject, not just self-reported enjoyment14. The four flashbacks were also made harder to miss in the September 2026 revision — a screen-flash, a numbered "idea 2 of 4" progress dial, and a gold tag now mark each one as a named learning beat rather than a scene transition.

01 · Before Act 0

The opening choice, and the clock underneath the story

On screen, first

A title card with two cards side by side: Quantum Heist (the full story below, start to finish) and Quantum Playground (skip straight to the seven-card open-ended sandbox, §07 — renamed from "Quantum Realm," same destination, much larger menu). The Playground starts no clock and touches none of the story state — it's a complete, self-contained activity, not a locked "advanced mode." Point anyone short on time, or repeating the session, at it directly.

On screen, once Quantum Heist is chosen

VALE — "You're in. Good. Behind that door is a vault floor with three rooms, and in the last one is a ledger that puts four people in prison. I can't open any of it. You can."

YOU — "I have never written a line of code in my life."

VALE — "You will have, in about twenty minutes. Touch that door and the alarm starts. Six minutes from then."

The mechanics

The clock is 360 seconds and only starts once "Touch the door" is pressed. Every action costs time: opening a box by hand costs 4s, a search step costs 8s, a missed room attempt costs 20s, each ledger account read costs roughly 1s. Trying different shift numbers on the first door — and the until-loop door right after it — costs nothing at all; both are deliberately free to experiment with. Every deduction pulses the clock and pops a small floating "−Ns" tag, so the cost actually registers. If time would go negative, Okonjo buys ninety seconds once, with a toast — and if it would go negative again, Vale buys a further thirty seconds, once. If a learner needs still more rescuing than that, an open-ended pool of randomised, non-repeating in-fiction excuses each buy a further 20–39 seconds, indefinitely — the clock cannot go negative on any playthrough, however slowly someone works. The debrief stays light for a clean run ("Out, with [time] to spare.") versus a rescued one ("Out — by the skin of your teeth."), and names how many close calls happened.

⏱ Timing reality check

Six minutes is a narrative device, not a session-length promise. A facilitated run — reading dialogue aloud, letting people actually think through each picker, pausing at flashbacks, running the quiz — comfortably takes 20–35 minutes. Don't let the on-screen clock set your pace. See §09.

02 · Exhibit A

The Outer Door

Teaches: variable, until loop. Wrapped in: a shift cipher.

On screen

Intercepted combination: CHBSA. Vale: "It's a shift cipher. Every letter moved along the alphabet by the same amount… nobody knows how far. That number is the whole secret."

The concept: variable

A named location that holds a value you're allowed to change — that's the whole definition. The pedagogical point is naming, not mechanics: x = 7 and shift = 7 run identically and read completely differently three weeks later.

◈ FILE 1-AAnswer key — the decoder▸

Name the box — shift / shift_amount = correct (solid green). x / thing = accepted, with a tip — a dashed green border and a 💡 mark, not amber (amber was dropped; testers read it as a rejection when the actual message is "this works, here's a style tip"): "it runs, but in a month nobody reading x knows what x is."

Put a number in it — options are 3, 5, 7, 11, 19, explicitly framed as free guesses. Only 7 is correct — the secret word is VAULT shifted by 7 to give CHBSA. Any other number decodes to gibberish, and costs nothing — the per-guess time cost was removed to match the until-loop door right after it, which never charged per guess either.

◈ FILE 1-BAnswer key — the until loop▸

Second door, secret word LEDGER shifted by 14. The picker accepts any option — that's deliberate, "code as thought" lets a wrong choice run so its failure is instructive — but only one combination actually opens the door:

◆ Technical accuracy note

A shift (Caesar) cipher is a teaching prop, not real cryptography — 25 possible keys, broken in antiquity. The "real encryption" claim belongs to the closing screen (§06) and is about modern public-key cryptography, a completely different problem. Keep the two separate in your own explanations.

◆ The career thread — Data Analyst

The job-complete screen after this task names Data Analyst. Low-tech-sounding on purpose: naming a variable sensibly and looping until a query has what it needs is the daily texture of that job, not a stretch analogy. If asked for the exact line: "every dashboard a bank runs starts with a named variable holding today's number, and a loop that keeps pulling records until the query is done."

03 · Exhibit B

Room One

Teaches: sequence / linear search. Introduces: superposition, measurement, interference, the first real Grover step.

On screen

"The key to room two is in one of those four. Open them. That is all you can do — and it is all the house computer can do either." Then: "A coin on the table is heads or tails. Spin it and ask again — while it spins it is not secretly one of them. It is genuinely both."

Linear search (classical)

Opening boxes one at a time from the top is a linear search — what every ordinary computer does to find an unindexed item, billions of times a second. Worst case for 4 boxes: 4 opens.

Superposition and measurement

A qubit can hold a weighted mix of 0 and 1 at once; measuring it forces one definite outcome, collapsing that mix5. The spinning-coin analogy is fair provided you don't push it further: don't say a qubit "is" both 0 and 1 — say it has a probability of being found in either state until measured.

◆ The trap this activity avoids

Spinning all four boxes and grabbing one still gives 25% odds — the activity says so explicitly ("lovely, and useless"). Superposition alone searches nothing. This matters because it's set up before the actual mechanism, not glossed over.

Interference — the real mechanism (Mark + Boost)

The most common wrong popular explanation of quantum computing is "it tries every answer at once." This build is careful not to teach that. The real mechanism is amplitude amplification67: Mark flips the phase of the correct answer's amplitude; Boost reflects every amplitude about the average, growing the marked one and shrinking the rest. Repeat, and the correct answer's probability climbs toward certainty. Superposition creates possibilities; interference is what steers the measurement toward the right one.

⚡ Flash-forward — "You just ran Grover's algorithm"

Right after this win, a full-screen "flash-forward" names the mechanism outright. Its wording was substantially simplified in a later revision, after feedback that the original was too dense for both children and adult learners with zero tech background — the facts didn't change, only the language: "Spin, mark, boost, look. What you just did has a real name: Grover's algorithm, worked out by a scientist called Lov Grover in 19968. It's a genuine way to search through a big pile of unsorted things — and it beats checking one at a time." One concrete before/after number replaces the earlier "quadratically faster" phrasing — "Check a million things one at a time: a million steps. Hand the same search to a quantum computer: about a thousand steps" — then it closes on a single concrete stakes example (banks already exploring this exact idea for one suspicious transaction out of millions — tying directly into the Ledger task next) rather than a list of three, tying back to the same bank research-team claim below15. Same overlay mechanics as the four flashbacks (typewriter reveal, tap to finish, a progress dial, a back button) but on a cool cyan/violet theme with its own sound cue and a confetti burst, so it reads as a bigger, more electric beat than the calm sepia-toned flashbacks.

◆ The career thread — Quantum Computing Researcher

The job-complete screen after this task names Quantum Computing Researcher, paired with a checkable claim: "Several major banks — HSBC, Barclays, Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan among them — now run dedicated quantum computing research teams." Accurate as of the most recent sector survey15: HSBC has a Group Head of Quantum Technologies and has trialled quantum-enabled algorithmic trading with IBM and Quantinuum; Barclays has run quantum research since 2017 out of its Chief Technology Office; Goldman Sachs runs a named quantum research team; JPMorgan Chase has demonstrated certified quantum randomness and QAOA speedups and operates its own quantum-secured inter-datacentre network. None of this means Grover's algorithm is in production anywhere today — it isn't — only that the research capability named is real and current, not aspirational.

Classical vs quantum, stated precisely

Worst-case classical is N. Grover needs on the order of √N oracle calls — this is a quadratic speedup, not exponential, and it is provably optimal: no quantum algorithm can search an unstructured list faster than roughly √N queries8. Quadratic sounds modest next to popular quantum-computing hype; it is genuinely what the published mathematics says, and the honest framing is what makes this activity trustworthy.

◆ The career thread — Automation Engineer

The job-complete screen after this task names Automation Engineer. A direct link, not an analogy: anywhere a bank repeats a known number of steps a known number of times — reconciling accounts, end-of-day batch jobs, retrying a failed payment a fixed number of times before escalating — a counted loop is doing the work a person used to do by hand. No citation needed; it's a plain description of what the job is.

◆ The = / == / != picker, redesigned

An earlier version only explained this via a one-line legend plus a beat that fired after a right or wrong guess — feedback was that it wasn't landing, and part of the problem was the picker's own layout: a single row labelled "COMPARISONS" had the assignment operator = sitting in the middle of it, undermining the very distinction being taught. The picker is now built around the distinction itself: a plain-language line sits above both groups — "= is a label maker — it sticks a value onto something and changes it. == and != don't change anything at all; they just ask a yes-or-no question. Only a question belongs in an if." — followed by two visually separate groups: ASKS A QUESTION (> < == !=, "true or false — nothing changes") and SETS A VALUE (= alone, its own accent colour, "not a question — changes what's stored"). The same wording now appears in three places a learner meets it — this picker, the pre-Ledger flashback, and the in-puzzle beat — rather than three different explanations of the same thing.

The oracle, made literal

The build's own line does real technical work, not just narrative flourish: "You did not learn a second language. You wrote one rule, and handed it to a different machine… Your if became the Mark. That is all an oracle is: a rule that recognises the answer when it sees it." A Grover oracle is a subroutine that flips the phase of any state satisfying a predicate — exactly the boolean condition already written, realised as a phase-flip instead of a True/False return6. This isn't a simplification for beginners; it's the literally correct relationship.

◈ FILE 4-BTask 4b — an actual period-finding mechanic▸

Flagging the account used to be the end of Task 4. It no longer is: pressing "Flagged. Now unmask the account →" moves to a new scene (s4b) where the learner is told the flagged account's real reference is masked behind a number, N = 91, and cracks it themselves rather than being told about it afterward — Shor's algorithm, not narrated this time but actually run, at a scale small enough to do by hand.

The mechanic: the learner repeatedly presses "Try the next power of 3," each press computing 3^x mod 91 and appending it to a visible chip sequence. The true sequence — verified independently before this was built — is 3, 9, 27, 81, 61, 1: six presses, 3 seconds each, until the remainder loops back to 1. That loop-length (6) is the period. The activity halves it (3), compares 3³−1 and 3³+1 against 91 — gcd(3³−1,91)=13, gcd(3³+1,91)=7 — and the two hidden factors fall out: 91 = 7 × 13, revealed with a decrypted routing code. No player choice or failure state, deliberately — like Mark+Boost in Room One, one new idea introduced guided, not a puzzle with wrong answers.

A genuine, correct worked example (a=3, N=91=7×13, r=6), chosen and verified before any of this was built precisely because it's classroom-tractable by hand while still being real arithmetic. The build is explicit about the scaling gap, in the beat that fires once the pattern is found: "By hand, you multiplied this out step by step; a real bank reference number is hundreds of digits longer, and 'step by step' stops being an option long before you get there."

⚡ Flash-forward — "You just found a period. That is Shor's algorithm."

Once Task 4b is complete, a second flash-forward fires right before the closing screen — this is where the Grover-≠-Shor distinction is made explicitly, on screen, by name. Unlike the first draft of this flash-forward (written before the period-finding mechanic existed), it now ties directly to what the learner just did: "Worth being precise here: the shift cipher on that first door was brute force, not quantum — you just tried every shift until one made a word. And Grover's algorithm, the search you ran twice tonight, does not break encryption either; at best it roughly halves the effective strength of a key." It then names what the learner just did by hand — hunting for where 3^x mod 91 looped back to 1, then splitting that loop-length in half to reveal 91 = 7 × 13 — as the actual method behind Shor's algorithm, published by Peter Shor in 199417: "You did it by hand because 91 is tiny. A real encryption key is a number hundreds of digits long… A large enough quantum computer would not do anything different to what you just did; it would just find that same repeating pattern almost instantly, for a number that size." It closes on the same harvest-now-decrypt-later / post-quantum-migration framing as the sourced note below — a second, more detailed pass at the same warning, not a replacement for it.

⚠ Read before anyone technical asks

Grover's algorithm and "harvest now, decrypt later" are still not the same threat, even now that both are named — and one of them actually run — on screen. Grover gives a quadratic speedup against brute-force search — practically, symmetric keys (like AES) just need roughly double the length to stay safe, a known, manageable mitigation; the flash-forward now states this precisely ("at best it roughly halves the effective strength of a key") rather than leaving it implied. The far bigger threat behind harvest-now-decrypt-later is Shor's algorithm, which gives an exponential speedup at factoring — the hard problem today's public-key cryptography (RSA, ECC) relies on. If asked "is this Shor's algorithm?" — yes, in the sense that matters: the learner performed real period-finding arithmetic by hand, on a genuine worked example (a=3, N=91=7×13, r=6), which is precisely the classical arithmetic step Shor's algorithm accelerates. What the build does not claim is that a quantum computer was involved, or that this scales to a real key — it's explicit about both, and the direct search puzzles throughout the rest of the build (Rooms One–Three, the Ledger's scan) remain Grover's algorithm, unchanged. The underlying warning is correct regardless of which algorithm eventually does the breaking, and is now demonstrated as well as named.

"Save now, decrypt later" — sourced

  • The concept and its stakes for institutions with long data-confidentiality needs — exactly this finance-sector framing — are examined in a 2025 Federal Reserve working paper9.
  • NIST finalised the first three post-quantum cryptography standards — FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) — in August 2024, specifically to give organisations something to migrate to10.
  • The US NSA's CNSA 2.0 suite sets a concrete national-security-system migration timeline: mandatory by end of 203111. A US timeline, not a UK financial-services requirement — cite it as evidence of urgency, not as a rule that applies here.

07 · Optional module

The Quantum Playground — seven mini-labs

Not part of the story. Reachable from the opening card (§01) or the Big Picture screen (§06). No clock, no narrative — seven independent mini-labs, each pairing a classical machine against a quantum one on the same job, each showing both sides' real code, each closing on a named bank tech role.

What it is, why it's now seven cards, and its history

Every task in the main story is a fixed, single worked example — a deliberate scaffold (§00, worked-example effect), and scaffolds are meant to come off. The Playground is the un-scaffolded version: the learner sets the problem, watches a by-hand approach and a quantum approach run side by side, at whatever scale or configuration they choose, with both versions' actual code shown for direct comparison. This is learner-controlled, variable practice — rerunning an already-learned concept at a self-chosen scale, one of the better-evidenced ways to build a skill that transfers beyond the exact example it was learned on16.

◆ Fourth build, third full rebuild — and the biggest jump yet

Two earlier approaches — a build-it-yourself sandbox with staged construct introduction and a bidirectional Convert feature, then a full-screen circuit-canvas rebuild of that same drag-and-drop premise — were both tried and both explicitly rejected: assembling a program yourself, however presented, was the wrong fit for a closing activity. The third build removed the building entirely and landed as intended — unreserved positive feedback, plus the code panels and the extended 4–4096 range added on request. This round went further, on Mark's own ask: rename "Quantum Realm" to "Quantum Playground"; fold in the heist's own four programming constructs, not just the search comparison; add encryption/decryption; randomise the target instead of always fixing it at the last box; make every card always show a classical-vs-quantum visual and always name a real role; research and add one genuinely new quantum concept the heist doesn't cover anywhere else. The original head-to-head comparison — the third build's entire sandbox — is untouched at the code level and now sits in the menu as one card among seven, renamed Search; six new cards were added alongside it. The existing layout was also checked across five screen sizes/orientations before any of this was built on top of it — zero overflow, so the same full-screen shell was reused for all seven cards, and re-verified with the same sweep after the rebuild.

◆ How the randomised target works, and why it's not just cosmetic

Previously the target was always the last box in the wall, and the classical side always animated a full N-box scan regardless. Now, on every wall-based card (Search, Conditional, Encryption, and the quantum side of Until Loop/Variable), the target is genuinely randomised every run (randInt(N)) — and the classical wall animation stops the moment it reaches this run's randomised target, so the reported "this run" figure is honest and actually varies, not a relocated dot inside an animation that still ran to completion. The "worst case" figure alongside it stays a separate, honest caption that is always N, exactly as before. The point this sharpens: the quantum step count never varies with where the target happens to be, but the classical count does — a real difference, not just a randomised visual. All wall-based cards share the same underlying runClassicWall()/runQuantumWall() pair rather than each reimplementing this.

◈ FILE P-1Card 1 — Variable▸

On screen: a qubit-count slider (1–10) showing the equivalent register size (2ᵠ) live. "Load the variable / register" runs both sides: classical assigns x = 7, then reassigns x = 42 — genuinely only ever holds one thing. Quantum puts all 2ᵠ values into superposition at once, then measures, collapsing to one random value.

The point: a classical variable holds exactly one value, full stop; a quantum register in superposition genuinely holds many together until measured. Insight line: "that 'many values, held together' state is the raw material every quantum algorithm — including the search you ran tonight — works with, before interference steers it toward one answer."

Career tie-in — Quantitative Analyst: a classical risk model updates one market scenario at a time; banks exploring quantum computing are looking at holding many scenarios at once for Monte Carlo-style simulation — the same idea just demonstrated in miniature.

◈ FILE P-2Card 2 — Until loop▸

On screen: 16 boxes, one hidden key, re-randomised every run. Two independent buttons: "Guess until found" (classical — genuinely guesses in random order until it hits, 1–16 guesses) and "Commit & run" (quantum — a "steps to commit" slider, 1–8, is locked in before the run starts; disabled while it runs).

The point, and why the layout is asymmetric on purpose: classical can keep going for as long as it takes; quantum must commit to a step count in advance — reading the answer early would collapse the superposition. The insight line draws the direct parallel to Room One's own "press it twice and the odds fall" lesson: overshoot the committed count and, just like Mark+Boost pressed twice, the odds swing back down.

Career tie-in — IT Operations Engineer: a classical retry-until-it-works loop is exactly how much banking infrastructure recovers from a failed payment or dropped connection; quantum programs can't do that yet — every run today is a fixed, pre-planned job.

◈ FILE P-3Card 3 — Counted loop▸

On screen — the one card with no wall animation at all, by design: a pure, live odds calculator reusing the same odds-meter component as Room One and the Ledger. Two sliders — boxes (N, same eleven stops as elsewhere) and search steps (k) — drive a live odds % and fill bar, plus a "Jump to best k" button.

The point: drag k below the best value and the message says the odds haven't peaked yet; land on it and it states the real odds/ratio; overshoot it and it says so explicitly — "the odds have swung back down, exactly like pressing Mark + Boost twice in Room One" — the same overshoot lesson taught three different ways across this build.

Career tie-in — Automation Engineer: sizing a batch job by a repeat count is real, everyday work; unlike a classical batch job, overshooting a quantum search's step count actively makes the result worse, not just slower.

◈ FILE P-4Card 4 — Conditional▸

On screen: the learner picks a rule — "IF AMOUNT >/</== 10,000" — from a picker built like the Ledger's own operator picker (§05), then a size slider, then "Run the rule." Both sides run the shared wall animation with the code panels generated live to show the learner's own chosen rule baked into both the classical if and the quantum oracle call.

The point: the learner's own condition becomes the literal thing being searched for — the insight line states it outright: "the quantum oracle IS the condition you picked." The clearest card in the Playground for making "the oracle, made literal" (§05) land.

Career tie-in — Risk & Financial Crime Analyst: fraud/AML rules are exactly this shape — a condition flagging the one record out of thousands that breaks it.

◈ FILE P-6Card 6 — Encryption▸

On screen: a fixed "intercepted" ciphertext ("QGSNU VKPX ZLHOT"), a keyspace-size slider (4–4096), then "Run both": classical brute-forces keys one at a time, quantum runs its usual bestK(N)-step amplification. On completion the message decrypts to "THE SIGNAL IS CLEAR".

The point, stated honestly on screen: this demonstrates the mechanic of a keyed brute-force race, not a real cipher-breaking algorithm — it reuses the same generic wall search, relabelled. A note after running makes the real cryptographic point correctly: Grover only gives a quadratic speedup against brute-forcing a key like this, which is why symmetric keys just need to get longer; it's Shor's algorithm (the one already used against the Ledger's account number, Task 4b, §05) that actually threatens public-key encryption, which is why NIST finalised new post-quantum standards in August 202410. The same Grover-≠-Shor distinction this guide is careful about throughout — a second, hands-on place it gets made.

Career tie-in — Security / Cryptography Engineer: deciding how long a key needs to be, and against which attack, is real and current work — exactly why NIST's post-quantum standards exist.

◈ FILE P-7Card 7 — True randomness (QRNG) — the new concept▸

This is the genuinely new addition Mark asked for — a quantum idea the heist itself never touches anywhere else. Researched via web search, using the same outlet (The Quantum Insider) already cited for the banking career-thread claim15, so the sourcing style stays consistent.

On screen: a seed field (numeric, or any text — hashed if non-numeric) and "Generate 12 numbers" on the classical side; a second, unseeded "Generate 12 numbers" on the quantum side. Classical is a genuinely seeded, deterministic PRNG (mulberry32) — the same seed twice gives the same 12 numbers, which the on-screen line invites the learner to try and then confirms. Quantum has no seed field at all, because there's nothing to seed.

Honestly caveated, not overclaimed — read this before anyone technical asks. The build cannot reach real quantum hardware, and says so directly: "The numbers above all still come from your browser, not a real quantum chip — this page can't reach one." Under the hood, "quantum" stands in using Math.random(). The note correctly describes what a real QRNG exploits: "measuring a qubit in superposition is fundamentally unpredictable, not just hard to predict — no seed, no formula, nothing to replay, even in principle." Same honesty practice as Task 1's shift-cipher note.

The sourcing, verified and corrected for this guide. The on-screen citation reads "March 2026" — this is a one-year date error; the real dates are March 2025 and September 2024 (below). Two real claims sit behind the card's banking note: a certified-randomness demonstration (March 26, 2025) — JPMorgan Chase, Quantinuum, and three US national laboratories (Argonne, Oak Ridge, Lawrence Berkeley) plus UT Austin, published in Nature, 71,313 bits of validated entropy, more randomness generated than consumed18; and HSBC's tokenised-gold pilot (announced September 19, 2024) — Quantinuum's "Quantum Origin" quantum randomness deployed alongside post-quantum cryptography to guard against "store now, decrypt later" attacks19 — a genuine randomness claim, not only a post-quantum-cryptography one.

Career tie-in — Security / Cryptography Engineer (same role as the Encryption card, deliberately — randomness and encryption are two halves of the same job): every encryption key starts from a random number generator; a predictable one weakens every key built from it.

No clock and no wrong path out anywhere in the Playground — well suited to "free practice" time, or as its own standalone activity for someone who's already done the full heist. Every card resets to a fresh state on entry, so a second visit never opens on someone else's stale result. Nothing to get stuck on in any of the seven: no construct to assemble, nothing that can be built incorrectly.

Delivery notes

⏱
Realistic timing. A facilitated run with dialogue, discussion and the quiz takes 20–35 minutes, not six. Budget accordingly.
🔒
Locked gates are working as intended. If a button "isn't doing anything," check whether the builder above it is actually complete — every gate is genuinely disabled with a lock note until its condition is met.
🎯
"I picked an option and it didn't work" is a feature. In Task 1's loop and Task 3's step-count screens, the picker accepts any offered option — filling the blank isn't the same as it being functionally correct. That's only revealed on Run. This is "code as thought, not clicks" in practice.
📈
The top sticking point is "why isn't more always better?" Have the exact percentages from §04 ready — the live calculator is the fastest way to show it.
🟰
The second is = vs ==/!= in the Ledger. It's the most common real beginner bug in the world — say so, it's reassuring. The picker is now built around the distinction itself (two visually separate groups — "asks a question" vs. "sets a value," §05), so pointing at the two groups directly tends to land faster than re-explaining it verbally.
♿
Accessibility. Every interaction in the entire build — the main heist (Acts 0–5) and the seven-card Quantum Playground (§07) alike — is click/tap based; there is no drag-and-drop anywhere in the current build. The build respects prefers-reduced-motion. No external fonts or scripts, so it runs on locked-down machines.
🎬
Going deeper. The four flashbacks each carry a worked code example independent of the heist — the fastest way to show someone the idea in isolation if the story framing isn't landing for them personally. The Quantum Playground (§07) is the equivalent option for going deeper by doing rather than reading — seven independent open-ended cards.
